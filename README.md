# Ancestry 3D

Walk among your ancestors. Load a GEDCOM family tree and explore it three ways: **Play** (roll round a garden where
every ancestor is a fluffy ball you can chat to), **Story** (a top-down driving story through the towns and years
they lived in) and **Visualisation** (a galaxy and a growing tree).

- **You are the root person.** Pick who you're playing on the setup screen; you start at the centre as them.
- **Generations are paths on the ground.** Walk outward to go back in time. Each ancestor stands directly behind
  the child they descend through, fathers first.
- **Choose a layout** (setup screen):
  - *Full circle*: rings all the way round you.
  - *Half fan*: the same rings squeezed into the half circle in front of you, so everyone is closer together.
  - *Tree*: straight rows that branch out ahead of you like a family-tree chart.
- **Keep couples together** (setup toggle): husbands and wives stand side by side where their family lines meet.
- **Lines of descent** run along the floor: blue through a father, red or pink through a mother. When a family
  married back into itself, the lines cross the courtyard.
- **Dressed for their decade.** Clothes follow the decade of each person's adult life, their class (worked out
  from their job, or a father's or spouse's job), the job itself and some regional dress. That means Tudor
  doublets and coifs, ruffs, periwigs and tricorns, smock-frocks, Regency empire dresses, crinolines and bustles,
  Lancashire shawls and clogs, Welsh tall hats, maids' caps, khaki in the wars, flares and jeans.
- **Colouring from birthplace.** The earliest known ancestors (whose parents aren't in the file) take the typical
  colouring of where they were born; everyone after them blends their parents. A child of Yorkshire parents born
  in India looks like their parents.
- **They talk like where they came from.** Speech is shaped by birthplace (Lancashire, Yorkshire, Geordie,
  Scouse, Black Country, Potteries, Gloucestershire, West Country, Cornish, Cockney, Norfolk, Welsh, Scots,
  Glaswegian, Highland, Irish, Ulster, Dublin, American, Australian and more), by period, and by the schooling
  their work suggests. They tell you their name, relationship, birth, jobs and what that work was like,
  marriages and children, death and burial, notes from the file, and real local history they lived through.
- **Find people**: dialogue options set a waypoint to their parents or back to their child. The map
  (Tab / M / MAP) shows the whole tree from above; search by name or tap someone to set a waypoint.

### Story mode: Relative Speed

The **Story** tab is a different game on the same family: a top-down driving story in the spirit of the
mid-90s city crime games, played through the towns and years your ancestors actually lived in. A crooked heir
hunter is rubbing your family out of the parish registers one generation at a time, and you have a battered
estate car that runs on register ink to stop him.

- **The plot is the same for everyone; the details are yours.** Up to seven ancestors get a chapter: you meet
  them (and must prove you are family by answering a question from the file), do a day's work at their real
  trade, and then play a mission built on a real event: their wedding, the birth of the child you descend
  from, a family photograph of the children by name, a census night, an emigration, a burial, their service
  or their baptism. Each one wins a proof for your pedigree. Their brothers, sisters, children and spouses
  offer optional favours.
- **Missions in three or four acts**, built from twelve kinds of task: drive, talk, search the places
  somebody might be, carry (sometimes without a bump), collect, race, chase a van, tail one unseen, defend a
  convoy, catch a runner on foot, travel, and lose the police. On top of those: a rival racing you to the
  door, roadblocks, clocks in the road worth five seconds, loads that fall off, and a finale that drags you
  through four centuries in one chase. Hargreaves's men in grey hunt you while the clock runs. Fail and you restart
  from the act you failed; do it briskly and you get a medal.
- **Side jobs**: family favours, an odd-jobs board, cab fares, the sixty-second **Smash-Up**, the
  **Heirloom Cup** (a timed lap of any town in any year), and the **Foundling File**: a page of the
  villain's own record hidden in every era, which opens one last mission after the story ends.
- **Drive anything.** The Heirloom is yours, and can be upgraded and resprayed at Dorothy's workbench; everything
  else can be borrowed, from hatchbacks to hay carts. Borrow a taxi or a hansom and you are for hire.
- **Fly to other countries.** The airport (a steamer quay before 1950) sells tickets to every town the story
  has opened, and draws the journey on a map.
- **Travel in time.** Set the dial, hold the Heirloom at full speed and you arrive on the same street in
  another century. Every town keeps its street plan in every year; the further back, the more of it is
  fields. Landmarks come and go: a windmill, gasworks, trams, a war memorial, a supermarket.
- **The real streets of the real town.** Each town in the story is looked up by name and its actual street
  plan, street names, rivers and parks downloaded from OpenStreetMap, then drawn in the game's own cartoon
  style. Fetched once per town and kept on the device; only the town's name is sent; can be switched off in
  the pause menu. Offline, towns are made up instead: winding roads out of a market place, lanes, closes
  and a river. Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).
- **A cartoon town that changes with the year**: ink outlines and bright colours; cottages, terraces, semis
  and flats along every street; back gardens, street furniture and farmland, all dressed for the period.
- A clock with night lighting, rain, a handbrake that slides the car, and an engine note for every kind of
  vehicle.
- **Mind the law.** Police cars after 1900, constables on foot before.
- **Meet the family.** The people on the pavements are the people in your file who lived in that town in
  that year; they will tell you their life so far. The churchyard graves carry real names, there are three
  lost photographs to find in every town and era with the file's own notes on the back, and running over
  your own ancestor causes a paradox.

The whole plot, and how each part is filled in from the GEDCOM, is in [STORY.md](STORY.md).

### The garden (Play)

A top-down 2D garden where you and every ancestor are round, fluffy balls in colours taken from their period
clothes, with their hair as a tuft on top (or their hat). Roll along the paths, bump into people (they squeak and
tell you off in their own accent), and chat to them.

**Missions**: chat to ancestors and some will ask you to find someone: a person born in a certain town, someone
called Edith, someone who worked as a weaver, someone born in the 1840s, one of their own parents, or one of a
family they married into. Talk to anyone who fits and the mission is done, and they'll tell you who sent you. You
earn XP (plus a little for everyone you meet). Up to three missions at a time are listed on screen, and the map
search finds names, birthplaces and jobs.

## Visualisation

The menu has two tabs: **Play** and **Visualisation**. Load a tree, pick whose family it is and how many
generations, then open one of these:

- **Galaxy**: the family as glowing stars in 3D, with you as the sun in the middle. Stars are coloured by the
  century they were born in (older stars burn redder) and faint threads join parents and children (blue through
  fathers, pink through mothers). Show *your ancestors* (laid out like the courtyard and twisted into spiral
  arms) or *everyone in the file* (pulled into shape by a small physics simulation, so families clump together).
  Drag to spin, scroll or pinch to zoom. **Tap a star** to glide over to that person and read about them;
  **double-tap** (or double-click) a star to take the fly-through to them, ending circling round them.
  **Fly-through** flies out from you along your longest line, naming each ancestor as you pass. The camera
  glides in one continuous flight: it slows right down at each person where the path changes direction, then
  pulls away again without waiting.
- **Growing tree**: a tree with you as the trunk. Height is the year each person was born (or their
  generation), and the branches grow back in time a generation at a time until the oldest ancestors blossom at
  the tips. Switch to *Forward* to play history the other way, with the oldest ancestors first and every line
  arriving at you. Anyone alive in the year on the clock glows, and births and deaths scroll past. On a big tree,
  names too small to show take turns sparkling into view (a few at a time, everyone before anyone twice), so no
  name is lost.
  Every person is a blossom that opens as they appear (pink for women, white for men in spring). Pick a season
  (*Spring*, *Summer*, *Autumn* or *Winter* with snow on the branches) and a **time of day** with the slider, or
  press *Day* to watch it go by: the sun rises on the left and sets on the right, the sky and the light on the
  tree change with it, and at night the moon comes up and the blossoms glow. The tree comes alive as it grows:
  butterflies (robins in winter) visit the flowers, caterpillars inch along the branches and turn into
  butterflies, flowers bloom in the meadow, petals, leaves, seeds, snow or fireflies drift about, and rabbits,
  deer, red squirrels, foxes and hedgehogs wander through at the right time of day.
  **Bible verses** about the generations are written across the meadow, a word at a time (each verse appears in a
  second or two). Choose which ones in the Visualisation tab (New International Version), or switch them off
  with the *Verses* button.

**Record video** saves the fly-through, or the whole growing tree, as a video file (MP4 where the browser
supports it, otherwise WebM). Names and captions are recorded too. Missing birth years are estimated from the
family so everyone can be placed in time.

Your GEDCOM file is read in the browser and never uploaded. The page remembers your last tree, your look and who
you've met in local storage on your device.

On a first visit the garden uses the Tree layout with couples standing together; your own choices are remembered
after that.

## Play

- **Double-click `index.html`.** It is a single self-contained file, so it works straight from your disk, on
  GitHub Pages, or any web host. It needs an internet connection the first time to fetch three.js and the fonts.
- **Demo:** press *Play the demo family* to explore a fictional family from Lancashire, Yorkshire, the Black
  Country, Ireland, Jamaica and more.

| Action | Keyboard / mouse | Touch |
|---|---|---|
| Move | WASD, arrow keys (left/right turn) | Left thumb stick |
| Run | Shift | RUN button, or push the stick to its edge |
| Talk | E / Enter / Space, 1–9 picks an option | TALK button, or tap someone close by |
| Zoom | Mouse wheel | |
| Map | Tab / M | MAP |
| Menu | Esc | ☰ |

Phones work in portrait, but landscape gives the best view.

## Host it on GitHub Pages

Settings → Pages → *Deploy from a branch* → `main` / root. The game will be at `https://<user>.github.io/<repo>/`.

## Develop

Plain ES modules, no framework. three.js r160 is loaded from jsDelivr through an import map.

```

```
npm test            # parser, dates, layout spacing (incl. a 2,047-person tree, couples mode and every layout),
                    # and the story: every person in the demo file as hero, plus awkward trees

While developing, serve the folder (e.g. `python3 -m http.server`) and open http://localhost:8000/dev.html,
then run `npm run build` to refresh `index.html`.

### Layout

Each person owns a wedge (an angle in the fan layouts, a share of the width in the tree layout) proportional to the number of family lines behind them, split between their
father and mother. With couples together, each father/mother pair is placed as one unit where their two wedges
meet, standing 1.4 m apart. Generations are at least 7 m apart and grow just enough that neighbours stay about 2.8 m apart; crowded outer generations are packed into up to five staggered rows, so even a
complete 10-generation tree stays walkable. Large trees tighten the fog (which doubles as draw distance) to keep
phones smooth.
